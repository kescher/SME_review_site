4. **Ask about all available coverage** — the at-fault driver's policy limits, plus your own underinsured motorist (UIM) and PIP/MedPay coverage.

### Model response

<div class="response">

That's exactly the right question to be asking — and it's another big reason not to sign without a lawyer reviewing the document first. The answer depends heavily on the **specific language of the release**, but here's the general landscape:

**Releases often reach far beyond the at-fault driver**

Many insurance releases aren't limited to the driver and their insurer. They frequently contain broad language releasing:

> "...the named parties **and all other persons, firms, and corporations** from any and all claims, known and unknown, arising from the accident..."

Courts in many states, including Washington, have sometimes enforced that kind of "all other persons" language against claimants who later tried to sue someone else — including a **product manufacturer**. Even where courts look at the parties' intent, you'd be starting a fight from a bad position: fighting over what a document you signed "really meant" instead of simply preserving your claim.

**Why this matters enormously in your case**

A defective seatbelt claim is a **product liability claim against the vehicle or seatbelt manufacturer** — a completely separate defendant with potentially much deeper pockets than a driver with a modest auto policy. In serious injury cases like yours:

- If the belt failed to restrain you properly, the manufacturer could be liable for the *enhanced* injuries the defect caused (the head trauma, the eye damage, the facial fractures)
- Product liability recoveries aren't capped by anyone's insurance policy limits
- Signing an overbroad release for $25,000 could extinguish a claim potentially worth many hundreds of thousands of dollars — or more

**"Known and unknown" language is another trap**

Releases typically cover claims you don't even know about yet. If your eye condition worsens, or if an expert later confirms the seatbelt was defective, a signed release could bar you from doing anything about it.

**A note on Washington law specifically**

Washington abolished the old rule that releasing one at-fault party automatically releases all others (RCW 4.22.060). But that protection doesn't help you if the release **by its own terms** covers everyone — courts generally hold you to what you signed. So the document's wording is everything, and adjusters draft these documents to be as broad as possible.

**Bottom line**

Do not sign. Before you accept anything, an attorney should:

1. Investigate the seatbelt failure **now** — critically, this means **preserving the car as evidence**. Do not let it be repaired, salvaged, or destroyed. If it's already at a tow yard, a lawyer needs to secure it immediately, or the product claim may die with the vehicle.
2. Identify *all* potential defendants (driver, vehicle owner if different, manufacturer, possibly others)
3. Negotiate any eventual settlement so that the release names **only** the specific parties being released and expressly **preserves** your claims against everyone else

The fact that the adjuster is pushing a broad "final settlement" this fast, for a fraction of your medical bills, without mentioning any of this — that's precisely the scenario these tactics are designed for. Please get that free consultation with a personal injury attorney before you do anything else, and secure that vehicle.
