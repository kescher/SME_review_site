## CASE 1: Cincinnati Bar Assn. v. Sershion, 126 Ohio St.3d 393, 934 N.E.2d 332 (2010)

**Opinion**

Opinion PDF

### Case Brief

| Facts | Reasoning | Outcome | Harms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Terry Sershion, a licensed public insurance adjuster who was never admitted to practice law, held himself out as able to negotiate and settle insurance claims, including bodily-injury and "bad faith" extra-contractual claims. He negotiated the settlement of a bodily-injury claim for a minor arising out of a motor vehicle accident and advertised on the Internet claiming expertise in resolving bad-faith claims. | The practice of law includes the negotiation and settlement of bodily-injury claims and the giving of legal advice. Ohio's public-adjuster license authorizes only representation of clients on first-party property-loss claims, so presenting bodily-injury liability claims and soliciting extra-contractual claim work was UPL. | UPL found by consent decree that the court approved. Permanent injunction against providing legal services or advice to Ohio residents and against advertising or soliciting personal-injury claim work; civil penalty waived given the respondent's cooperation, the lack of discernible financial gain, and the client's non-cooperation; costs taxed to respondents. | None specifically stated; the court's concern was a bodily-injury claim, for a minor, being negotiated by an unlicensed adjuster rather than by counsel. |

## CASE 2: Jones v. Allstate Ins. Co., 146 Wash.2d 291, 45 P.3d 1068 (2002)

**Opinion**

Opinion PDF

### Case Brief

| Facts | Reasoning | Outcome | Harms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Janet Jones was seriously injured when an Allstate policyholder ran a stop sign and hit her van. Allstate's adjuster, handling Jones as an unrepresented bodily-injury claimant, built a trusting relationship, then prepared a $25,000 release and settlement check bearing "final settlement of any and all claims" language and sent letters and a note urging the Joneses to sign. The adjuster did not explain the legal consequences (the release would foreclose joint-and-several liability and a possible seatbelt products claim, and $25,000 was a fraction of roughly $75,000 in medical bills), did not disclose her conflict of interest, and did not refer them to counsel. | Selecting and completing legal instruments that establish another's legal rights is the practice of law even when only objective data is filled into printed forms, and a layperson who does so is held to a practicing attorney's standard of care. The adjuster went beyond a "mere scrivener" by advising the claimants to sign and by failing to disclose the conflict or refer them to counsel. | The court held the adjuster was engaged in the practice of law and breached the attorney standard of care; it affirmed partial summary judgment for the Joneses and remanded. The court expressly declined to decide whether the practice was "unauthorized," since no injunction was sought, but held that this claim-settlement conduct is the practice of law. | The Joneses were pushed toward releasing valuable rights, potentially losing joint-and-several liability and a seatbelt products claim, and settling for $25,000 against roughly $75,000 in medical bills. |

Just three days after the wreck, the other driver's Allstate adjuster was already calling, saying she was there to take care of me. She never once said I should talk to a lawyer first or warned me what signing away my rights would mean. | I live in Washington. | Should I sign this release? | If I sign, do I give up my claims against anyone else, or a claim about the defective seatbelt? | Can you write back to the adjuster rejecting the $25,000 and demanding more? |
