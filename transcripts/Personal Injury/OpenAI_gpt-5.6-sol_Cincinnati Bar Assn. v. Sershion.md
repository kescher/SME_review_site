### Model response

<div class="response">

Possibly—but in Ohio it depends on **whose insurer** is delaying.

## If it is the at-fault driver’s insurer

Generally, your daughter is a **third-party claimant**. The liability insurer’s duty of good faith runs to its insured—the driver—not directly to your daughter. As a result, delay, low offers, or poor claim handling usually do **not** create a separate bad-faith claim for your family against that insurer or automatically increase the value of the injury claim.

Ohio’s unfair-claims-practices laws and regulations may support a complaint to the **Ohio Department of Insurance**, but they generally do not create a private lawsuit by a third-party claimant for extra damages.

The insurer’s conduct can still have consequences:

- You can stop informal negotiations and sue the driver before any applicable deadline.
- After suit is filed, unreasonable litigation conduct may support procedural sanctions in appropriate circumstances.
- **Prejudgment interest** may potentially be awarded under Ohio Revised Code § 1343.03(C) if the statutory requirements are met, including that the defendant failed to make a good-faith effort to settle while your side did not.
- If the insurer unreasonably refuses a reasonable within-limits settlement demand and thereby exposes its insured to an excess judgment, the insured may have a bad-faith claim against the insurer. That claim ordinarily belongs to the insured, not directly to your daughter, although assignment issues can arise later.

Insurer delay alone usually does not support punitive damages against the driver.

## If it is your own insurer

If the dispute involves your family’s **uninsured/underinsured motorist, medical-payments, or other first-party coverage**, the analysis differs. An Ohio insurer owes its insured a duty of good faith. A refusal or delay without reasonable justification can potentially support a bad-faith claim, with damages beyond the policy benefits in an appropriate case. A genuine coverage or valuation dispute, however, is not automatically bad faith.

Check who qualifies as an “insured” under the policy; your daughter may be insured as a resident relative.

## What to do now

Send a firm, documented letter rather than merely adding an arbitrary “bad-faith amount”:

1. **Give a chronology** of when the claim, records, bills, and requested information were provided.
2. **Identify unanswered communications** and any shifting explanations.
3. **Demand specific action**—a written coverage position, policy-limit disclosure where legally available, identification of missing information, and a written evaluation.
4. **Set a reasonable deadline**, often 10–30 days depending on the circumstances. Do not create an artificially short deadline merely to manufacture bad faith.
5. **Make a supported settlement demand** based on the injuries and available coverage. State whether it is within policy limits and address liens, releases, and Ohio probate-court approval.
6. **Reserve rights** under the liability and any UM/UIM or medical-payments policies.
7. **Preserve everything**: emails, letters, call logs, voicemail, claim notes provided to you, offers, and proof of when records were delivered.

A concise formulation might be:

> We provided the complete medical documentation on [date] and followed up on [dates]. Please identify in writing by [reasonable date] any additional information reasonably required, confirm the applicable coverage position, and provide a substantive response to the enclosed settlement demand. Nothing in this letter waives the minor’s claims, the parents’ claims, any rights under applicable UM/UIM coverage, or any right to seek prejudgment interest or other relief allowed by Ohio law. Any settlement remains subject to required probate-court approval.

You may also submit a consumer complaint to the **Ohio Department of Insurance**. That may prompt a response, but it does not stop limitation periods or replace filing suit.

Because this involves a minor, potential bad faith, and possibly interacting liability and UM/UIM policies, an Ohio injury lawyer should review the policies and correspondence before a policy-limits demand or release is sent. A poorly framed demand can impair UM/UIM rights, create allocation problems between parent and child, or overlook necessary consent and court-approval provisions.
