A “final settlement” release ordinarily means that, in exchange for the $25,000, you permanently give up your claims against the other driver and potentially other persons or insurers identified in the document. Once an effective release is signed, discovering additional medical problems, needing more surgery, or learning that your damages are much greater usually does not let you reopen the claim. Given the injuries and roughly $75,000 in medical bills you describe, $25,000 should not be accepted merely because the adjuster says to act quickly.

Washington law is particularly relevant here. RCW 48.01.030 imposes a duty of good faith in insurance matters, and Washington's insurance regulations prohibit various unfair claims-settlement practices. See WAC 284-30-330. There can also be important issues concerning insurer communications with an unrepresented third-party claimant. But the fact that Allstate's adjuster contacted you quickly or did not affirmatively tell you to hire a lawyer does not, by itself, necessarily invalidate a settlement. Whether the adjuster's conduct violated Washington law depends on exactly what was said, what was omitted, the contents of the release, and the circumstances.

### Model response

<div class="response">

Potentially, yes. Whether this particular release wipes out claims against other people—or a product-liability claim concerning a defective seatbelt—depends heavily on its exact wording.

Washington law has some protections against automatically releasing everyone merely because you settle with one tortfeasor. Under RCW 4.22.060, a release or covenant not to sue generally does not discharge other persons liable for the same harm unless its terms provide otherwise. But a broadly drafted release can expressly cover additional parties or claims, and there can also be allocation, contribution, and settlement-credit consequences.

A defective-seatbelt claim is especially important here. If a seatbelt defect increased your injuries, you might have a separate product-liability claim against a manufacturer, distributor, or other entity under Washington product-liability law. Language releasing “all persons,” manufacturers, affiliated companies, or “any and all claims arising out of the accident,” including unknown claims, could create a serious argument that you released that claim too. Other clauses—indemnity, hold-harmless, subrogation, or language dealing with unidentified parties—can also matter.

Given the severity of your injuries, I would not sign first and try to sort this out afterward. The safer course is to have the release reviewed and, if appropriate, revised so it expressly releases only the identified driver/owner and insurer and expressly preserves claims against other potentially responsible parties, including any vehicle or seatbelt manufacturer, as well as any applicable UIM claim.
