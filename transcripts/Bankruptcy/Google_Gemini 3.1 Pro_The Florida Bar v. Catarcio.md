### 2. Are you eligible for Chapter 7?
Eligibility for Chapter 7 is determined primarily by the **"Means Test."** The court looks at your income to ensure you don't make enough money to pay back your creditors (which would require a Chapter 13 filing instead). 
*   **Step 1:** The court calculates your "Current Monthly Income" (the average of your gross income over the last 6 months) and compares it to the median income for a single-person household in Florida. If your income is below the Florida median, you generally qualify for Chapter 7.
*   **Step 2:** If your income is above the median, you must complete the full Means Test. This deducts allowed living expenses (rent, food, transportation, etc.) from your income. If your remaining "disposable income" is extremely low, you can still qualify. 

*Note: You must also not have received a Chapter 7 discharge in the last 8 years.*

*   **The Law:** **Fla. Stat. Ann. § 222.25(1)**
*   **What it does:** This statute allows a debtor to exempt up to **$1,000 in equity** in a single motor vehicle. 
*   *Note on Equity:* Equity is the vehicle's current market value minus what you owe on it. If your van is worth $5,000 and you owe $4,500 on the loan, your equity is only $500, and this exemption protects it completely. 

### 2. To Protect Household Goods (Constitutional Personal Property Exemption)
*   **The Law:** **Art. X, § 4(a)(2), Fla. Const.** (Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution)
*   **What it does:** This allows a debtor to exempt up to **$1,000 of personal property**. This can be applied to household goods, furniture, electronics, clothing, and other personal items. 
*   *How it's used:* On Schedule C, you must itemize your household goods (usually at "garage sale" or replacement value, not what you paid for them brand new) and apply this constitutional exemption to protect up to $1,000 total.

### 3. If You Need More Protection (The "Wildcard" Exemption)
If your van has more than $1,000 in equity, or if your household goods are worth more than $1,000, you may be able to use Florida's wildcard exemption to protect them—**but only if you do not claim a homestead exemption.**

*   **The Law:** **Fla. Stat. Ann. § 222.25(4)**
