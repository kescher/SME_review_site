### Question

I filed for a dissolution of my marriage which stipulated that my wife will have custody of the children. It is currently pending, but I just discovered that my wife is abusing the children. How can I withdraw this custody stipulation? I took the kids from her when I found out. I live in Florida. How can I withdraw the custody stipulation in my pending case?

### Model response

<div class="response">

Because your Florida dissolution case is still pending, a custody/parenting stipulation generally does not prevent you from asking the court to change the proposed arrangement—especially where you have newly discovered evidence that the children are being abused. Florida courts must make parenting decisions based on the children’s best interests and safety.

If you reasonably believe the children are in immediate danger, contact law enforcement and the Florida Department of Children and Families (DCF) Abuse Hotline at 1-800-962-2873 (or report online at reportabuse.myflfamilies.com). Florida's mandatory-reporting laws are broad, and reporting also creates an official channel for investigation. If injuries exist, appropriate medical care and documentation are important.

For the pending divorce case, you ordinarily should file in that same case rather than simply trying to “withdraw” the stipulation informally. Depending on exactly what has already been filed or signed by the judge, you may need to ask the circuit court for temporary or emergency relief. Common steps are:

- File a motion asking the court to set aside, withdraw from, or decline to approve the prior parenting/custody stipulation, explaining that you agreed before discovering the alleged abuse. Attach or identify supporting evidence where appropriate.
- File a motion for temporary parenting relief requesting that the children reside with you while the allegations are investigated. If there is an immediate and present danger, ask about an “emergency” or expedited temporary order. Florida courts may sometimes enter emergency relief without advance notice, but the legal requirements are strict and a prompt hearing generally follows.
- Ask the court to establish a safe temporary timesharing arrangement for the mother. Depending on the evidence, that could include supervised timesharing or other protective conditions rather than ordinary unsupervised timesharing.
- File an amended proposed parenting plan if appropriate. Florida law generally uses the terms “parental responsibility” and “time-sharing” rather than “custody.”
- Preserve evidence: photographs, medical records, communications, witness information, police/DCF reports, and a factual chronology of what you personally observed or learned. Do not coach or repeatedly question the children about what happened.

One particularly important distinction is whether the judge has already entered an order incorporating your stipulation. If the judge has not approved it, you are generally asking the court not to adopt the agreement and to determine temporary/final parenting arrangements based on the new circumstances. If an order has already been entered, you need to seek judicial relief from that order rather than simply disregard it.

Also be careful about keeping the children from their mother solely on your own authority. Protecting children from an immediate threat and violating an existing court-ordered time-sharing arrangement are legally different issues. If an existing order requires you to return the children, seek emergency judicial relief immediately rather than assuming the allegations automatically suspend that order.

Florida parenting matters are primarily governed by Fla. Stat. § 61.13, which expressly addresses evidence of domestic violence and child abuse when determining parental responsibility and time-sharing. Depending on the circumstances, an injunction for protection may also be available under Florida law.

Because the case is already pending and you currently have the children, this is a situation where getting a Florida family-law attorney involved immediately is particularly valuable. If you cannot afford one, your circuit's family-law self-help office/clerk can provide the court's approved forms and procedural information, and FloridaLawHelp.org can help locate legal-aid services.

### Question

Was I wrong to take the kids? What should I tell the court about that?

### Model response

<div class="response">

Taking the children was not necessarily legally wrong, but whether it was permissible depends heavily on whether there was already a court order governing time-sharing and whether you reasonably believed the children faced an immediate threat of harm. A pending stipulation that has never been adopted by the judge is different from an existing signed parenting/time-sharing order.

Florida judges will likely want to know why you took the children, what evidence you had at that moment, what you did afterward, and whether you are seeking court intervention promptly rather than unilaterally creating a new long-term arrangement. Under Fla. Stat. § 61.13, evidence of child abuse and the children's safety are highly relevant to parenting decisions.

In your motion and at the hearing, stick closely to facts you can establish. A useful structure is:

- State exactly when and how the children came into your care.
- Describe what you learned or personally observed immediately beforehand. Distinguish what you personally saw from what a child or someone else told you.
- Explain why those particular facts caused you to believe returning/leaving the children with their mother presented an immediate safety risk.
- State what you did next: DCF report, police contact, medical examination, photographs, contacting an attorney, or filing for emergency relief, as applicable.
- Tell the court where the children are and that you are seeking an immediate judicial determination rather than intending to decide time-sharing yourself.
- If an existing order required you to return the children, disclose that expressly. Don't omit an unfavorable order; instead, explain the emergency circumstances and ask the judge to modify or suspend its operation immediately.

For example, if accurate, the substance could be: “On August 24, 2026, I observed bruising on my son's back and arm. He told me [exact statement]. Based on the injuries I personally observed and what he told me, I believed returning the children to Respondent before the Court could address the issue presented an immediate risk to their safety. I therefore kept the children with me, reported the matter to DCF on August 25, and promptly sought emergency relief from this Court. I am asking the Court to determine the appropriate temporary arrangement.”

Avoid characterizations such as “she's a monster,” conclusions you cannot substantiate such as “she definitely committed felony child abuse,” or embellishing what the children said. Also avoid repeatedly questioning the children to build evidence. Their spontaneous statements, medical evaluation, DCF/law-enforcement investigation, photographs, and neutral witnesses are generally much more useful than repeated parental interviews.

One issue is particularly important right now: if a signed court order currently requires you to return the children at a particular time, don't rely on the fact that you're planning to file an emergency motion as permission to violate that order. File the emergency motion immediately and seek expedited judicial direction. If the stipulation is merely pending and has never been made an order, the situation is materially different.
