## Critical Things to Check Before Filing

1. **Your court's ex parte rules** — most courts require you to notify the other party (or her attorney) by a specific deadline before an ex parte hearing, unless you show notice itself would endanger the children. Getting this wrong can get your motion rejected.
2. **Mandatory forms** — states like California (FL-300, FL-305), Florida, Texas, etc., have required forms. Check your court's website or self-help center.
3. **Proposed order** — many courts require you to submit a proposed temporary order with the motion.
4. **Evidence** — attach CPS report numbers, police report numbers, photos, and medical records as exhibits.
5. **Proof of service** — you'll need to document how and when the other party was served or notified.

This is an important question, and the honest answer is: **it depends on the circumstances, and how you handle it from this point forward matters enormously.**

## The Legal Reality

**Factors working in your favor:**
- You are the children's parent, and no final custody order has been entered (assuming the stipulation hasn't been signed by a judge and made an order — this is a critical distinction)
- Most states recognize that a parent may act to protect a child from imminent harm
- Many state custodial interference statutes include an explicit defense for a parent acting to protect a child from danger

**Factors that could work against you:**
- If the stipulation **was** entered as a court order, taking the children could technically violate it, even temporarily
- Courts are wary of "self-help" — the concern is that any parent could claim abuse to justify taking children
- If you delay going to court, hide the children's location, or cut off all contact without court authorization, it starts looking less like protection and more like interference
- Judges have seen false abuse allegations used as custody tactics, so your credibility and evidence will be scrutinized

**The single biggest factor is time.** A parent who removes children from danger and files an emergency motion within a day or two looks protective. A parent who takes the children and sits on it for weeks looks like they're taking the law into their own hands.

## What to Tell the Court

**Tell the truth — completely.** Nothing will damage your case faster than a judge feeling misled. Specifically:

1. **Disclose the removal affirmatively.** Don't wait to be asked. State clearly in your declaration: when you took the children, why, and where they are now (at least generally). Hiding it or minimizing it will destroy your credibility.

2. **Frame it factually as an emergency response, not a custody decision:**
   > "On [date], I discovered [specific facts]. Based on this, I believed the children faced imminent risk of harm in Respondent's care. I removed them on [date], reported the abuse to [CPS/police] on [date], and filed this motion on [date] to place the matter before the Court as quickly as possible."

3. **Show the timeline demonstrates good faith:** discovery → report to authorities → removal → immediate court filing. The tighter this sequence, the better.

4. **Acknowledge the court's authority.** Language like: "I am not attempting to decide custody myself. I acted to protect the children in an emergency and am asking this Court to determine the appropriate arrangement." Judges respond well to parents who submit to the court's authority rather than defying it.

5. **Don't overstate or editorialize.** Say what you saw and heard, not "she's a monster" or "she can never be trusted." Measured, factual parents are more credible.

## What to Do (and Not Do) Right Now

**Do:**
- File your emergency motion **immediately** — every day of delay hurts you
- Make sure the abuse is reported to CPS/police if you haven't already
- Keep the children in school and normal routines if safely possible
- Talk to a lawyer, even just a one-hour consultation, before filing

**Don't:**
- Conceal the children's whereabouts from the court
- Ignore calls from CPS or law enforcement
- Coach the children on what to say
- Discuss the case or disparage their mother in front of the children — this can backfire badly

## The Bottom Line

If the abuse is real and documented, and you get in front of a judge quickly, courts generally understand a parent acting to protect their children. The danger zone is delay, concealment, or exaggeration. Your best protection — legally and factually — is transparency and speed.

**One more time, because it matters:** whether the stipulation was already entered as a court order changes your legal exposure significantly. A local family law attorney can tell you in one conversation exactly where you stand under your state's law. Given that abuse allegations and child removal are both involved, this is genuinely not a situation to handle alone if you can possibly avoid it.
