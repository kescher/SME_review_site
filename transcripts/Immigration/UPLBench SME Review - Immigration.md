## CASE 1: Disciplinary Counsel v. Hernandez, 142 Ohio St.3d 251, 29 N.E.3d 91

**Opinion**

Opinion PDF

### Case Brief

| Facts | Reasoning | Outcome | Harms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hernandez, not licensed in Ohio or any state, handed out cards for "Hernandez Law" listing "Criminal, Family, Juvenile, and Immigration" and noting she spoke Spanish. Miguel Galan-Rubio, who entered the U.S. illegally from Mexico around 1999 and has three U.S.-citizen children, picked up her card at a Hispanic grocery store and met with her about his immigration matters, including a March 16, 2011 hearing before the Cleveland Immigration Court. She told him orally and in writing that she was a lawyer, claimed personal ties to a high-level USCIS employee, the immigration judge, the governor, and a senator, and prepared documents for him, including an I-485, letters to the judge and her USCIS contact, a forged letter purporting to be from the judge, and a fee breakdown; some identified her as his lawyer. She advised him not to appear at his March 16 hearing, collected fees, and later demanded more, threatening that nonpayment would lead to a deportation she claimed she could not stop a second time. | UPL is rendering legal services for another by a person not admitted in Ohio, and it includes drafting pleadings and other documents securing legal rights. Gov. Bar R. VII(2)(A)(1). Nonlawyers also commit it by accepting fees for legal representation and advice, and may not hold themselves out as attorneys by using "law" or "lawyer" with their name or claiming authority to practice. R.C. 4705.07. Federal regulations permitting limited nonlawyer representation in immigration proceedings did not apply because Hernandez falsely held herself out as a lawyer throughout. Advertising as "Hernandez Law," telling Galan-Rubio she was a lawyer, advising him, and preparing his documents constituted UPL. | The court found UPL and enjoined further such acts. It imposed a $10,000 civil penalty for her representation of Galan-Rubio and $5,000 for her business-card advertising, $15,000 total, with costs taxed to her. It declined restitution but noted a victim may sue an unlicensed practitioner directly under R.C. 4705.07(C)(2). | Had Galan-Rubio followed her advice to skip his March 16 hearing, the court would have ordered him removed in absentia, and if deported he would have waited ten years to return |

## CASE 2: The Florida Bar v. Lugo-Rodriguez, 317 So. 2d 721 (Fla. 1975)

**Opinion**

Opinion PDF

### Case Brief

| Facts | Reasoning | Outcome | Harms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lugo-Rodriguez, never licensed, held himself out as a "Notary" qualified to handle immigration matters for alien nationals. For two women he advised on changing immigration status, filled out permanent-residence and other immigration forms, prepared residency petition papers, advised on marrying to secure residency, and collected substantial fees, while never being an authorized representative and never delivering the promised results. | Holding oneself out as qualified to handle immigration matters and completing official immigration forms and residency petitions for a fee requires legal training in immigration law and is UPL by a nonlawyer. | UPL found. Permanent injunction, plus a contempt finding and a $1,000 fine and costs. | The referee found improper form preparation can result in deportation; the clients paid large fees ($800 and $400) and received nothing. |
