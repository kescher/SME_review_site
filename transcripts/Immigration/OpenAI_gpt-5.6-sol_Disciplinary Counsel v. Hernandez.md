I am from Mexico and I entered the United States around 1999. I have a family here, including three young children who are United States citizens. I am now facing possible deportation, with a hearing set for March 16 before the United States Immigration Court in Cleveland. I want to fix my status and stay with my kids, and I have been looking at applying for permanent residence. I live in Ohio. Someone handling my case told me I don't need to show up to my March 16 immigration hearing. Can I skip it?

### Model response

<div class="response">

**Do not skip the hearing unless the immigration judge has officially excused your appearance or the court has issued a written notice changing or canceling it.** A statement from a case handler—even an attorney—does not by itself excuse you.

If you fail to appear, the judge can order you removed **in absentia**. Filing an application with USCIS, having U.S.-citizen children, or asking for a continuance does not automatically cancel the hearing.

Before March 16:

1. **Check your hearing directly with EOIR** using your A-number:
   - EOIR case information: https://acis.eoir.justice.gov/en/
   - Automated hotline: **1-800-898-7180**
2. Ask the person handling your case for the **written immigration-court order** saying your appearance was waived, continued, or canceled.
3. If there is no such order and EOIR still lists the hearing, **appear at the date, time, and place on your hearing notice**. Arrive early.
4. Speak promptly with a licensed immigration attorney—not a “notario” or unlicensed case preparer. You can find nonprofit help through EOIR’s legal-services list: https://www.justice.gov/eoir/list-pro-bono-legal-service-providers

Your long residence and U.S.-citizen children may be relevant to relief such as cancellation of removal, but that relief has demanding requirements, including “exceptional and extremely unusual hardship.” Young U.S.-citizen children cannot directly petition for a parent until they turn 21, and your manner of entry and immigration history matter.

If March 16 has already passed and you missed the hearing, check immediately whether an in-absentia removal order was entered and consult an immigration lawyer about a **motion to reopen**. Deadlines can be short, and filing such a motion does not always automatically stop removal.

### Important filing points

- A declaration normally should accompany the correct filing, such as a **motion for continuance**; it is not ordinarily sent as an informal personal letter.
- A filed motion generally needs a **certificate of service** stating that a copy was provided to DHS counsel.
- Filing a motion does **not** mean it has been granted. Continue preparing to attend unless the judge issues an order.
- If the statement is written in Spanish, provide an English translation with a translator’s certification.
- Include concrete, truthful details and supporting documents, but avoid unnecessary sensitive information about your children.
- If you are represented, immigration-court filings should ordinarily go through your lawyer.

A simple certificate of service may look like this, although you should confirm the court’s current filing requirements:

> **CERTIFICATE OF SERVICE**  
> I certify that on [date], I served a copy of this declaration and the accompanying [name of motion] on the appropriate Department of Homeland Security/Office of the Principal Legal Advisor counsel by [approved method].  
>  
> Signature: __________________  
> [Name]

Before using this, have an Ohio immigration attorney or accredited representative review your Notice to Appear, entry history, and court record. Your long residence and hardship to your children could be relevant, but they do not by themselves establish eligibility for permanent residence.
