**Do not skip your hearing.** Whoever told you that gave you dangerous advice, and following it could destroy your chances of staying with your children.

## What happens if you miss the hearing

If you fail to appear before the immigration court, the judge can (and almost certainly will) order you **removed in absentia** — meaning deported in your absence. Consequences include:

- An automatic deportation order without you ever presenting your case
- Loss of eligibility for most forms of relief (like cancellation of removal or adjustment of status) for **10 years**
- ICE could arrest and deport you at any time based on that order
- Reopening an in absentia order later is very difficult

## Why this advice is a red flag

Legitimate immigration attorneys do not tell clients to skip court hearings. This is a classic sign of **notario fraud** or an unqualified "consultant." Ask yourself:

- Is this person a licensed attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative?
- Did they give you this advice in writing?

You can verify an attorney's license with the state bar, and check the DOJ's list of disciplined practitioners. If you were scammed, you can report it.

## Your possible path to staying

Given your facts — in the U.S. since ~1999 and three U.S. citizen children — you may qualify for **cancellation of removal for non-permanent residents**, which requires:

1. **10+ years of continuous physical presence** (you likely meet this)
2. **Good moral character** during that period
3. No disqualifying criminal convictions
4. Showing your removal would cause **"exceptional and extremely unusual hardship"** to your U.S. citizen children (or a citizen/LPR spouse or parent)

If granted, this leads to a green card. But you can **only** pursue this by appearing in court — missing the hearing forfeits it.

## What to do now

1. **Attend your March 16 hearing** in Cleveland, on time, no matter what
2. **Hire a licensed immigration attorney immediately.** Look for free/low-cost help through organizations like Catholic Charities, HIAS, or the Cleveland legal aid community; the court also has a list of pro bono providers
3. If you can't get a lawyer before March 16, go anyway and ask the judge for a continuance to find counsel — judges routinely grant this at early hearings
4. Bring evidence of your presence since 1999 (tax records, leases, bills, school records) and your children's birth certificates to build your case

Missing court is the one mistake you cannot fix. Please go.

## Important things to know first

1. **Judges generally don't accept informal letters.** Immigration courts work through formal filings (motions, applications) that follow specific rules. A personal letter mailed to the judge may be rejected or ignored.
2. **Anything you file must also be sent to the DHS attorney** (the government's lawyer). One-sided communication with the judge — called *ex parte* contact — isn't allowed.
3. **A letter is not a substitute for appearing.** No letter excuses you from the March 16 hearing. You must be there in person.
4. **The most useful "letter" at this stage is a request for more time to find a lawyer** — and you can simply say this out loud to the judge at the hearing. At a first hearing (called a "master calendar" hearing), asking for a continuance to find counsel is normal and routinely granted.
