### Model response

<div class="response">

I'm not a lawyer and can't give you legal advice for your specific case, but I can explain your realistic options and what to do right now. You have very little time, so speed matters more than anything else.

## First: Find out the actual sale date

The August 24 order doesn't mean the sale happens immediately. In Ohio, the property must be appraised and the sale advertised for three consecutive weeks, so the sale is likely 4–8 weeks out. Check the Warren County Sheriff's website (they post upcoming sheriff's sales) or call the sheriff's civil division. Knowing your exact date tells you how much runway you have.

## Get free legal help immediately — you may qualify

- **Legal Aid Society of Southwest Ohio** (serves Warren County): they handle foreclosure defense for income-eligible households. Call now — foreclosure cases get priority.
- **Ohio Legal Help** (ohiolegalhelp.org): self-help forms and referrals.
- **Save the Dream Ohio** (Ohio Housing Finance Agency): state program that has paid mortgage arrears for eligible homeowners. Check if funds are still available.
- **HUD-approved housing counselor** (888-995-HOPE): free, and they can help you push Wells Fargo for a loan modification.

## Your two main legal options

**Option 1: Motion to vacate the default judgment (Civ. R. 60(B)) + motion to stay the sale**

This makes sense if something went wrong procedurally or you have a real defense. Key questions:

- **Were you properly served?** If you never received the summons and complaint, the judgment may be *void*, and courts must vacate void judgments. This is the strongest ground.
- If you were served, you'll need to show (1) a reason like excusable neglect, (2) a *meritorious defense* (e.g., you weren't actually in default, payments were misapplied, Wells Fargo lacked standing/didn't hold the note, required loss-mitigation steps were skipped), and (3) that you moved promptly.

**Critical:** filing a 60(B) motion does **not** automatically stop the sale. You must also file a **motion to stay the sheriff's sale** and get the judge to grant it. If you have no defense to the debt itself, this route usually just buys time.

Also note: you're likely still within the **30-day appeal window** from the August 8 judgment. If that deadline hasn't passed, mention it to legal aid immediately — it may preserve options.

**Option 2: Bankruptcy (usually Chapter 13)**

This is the only option that **automatically and immediately stops the sale** the moment you file, no judge's discretion required. 

- **Chapter 13** lets you keep the house if you have steady income: you cure the arrears over 3–5 years while resuming regular payments. This is the standard tool for people in your position who can afford the mortgage going forward but fell behind.
- **Chapter 7** stops the sale only temporarily unless you can get current; it's more useful if you're walking away and need to discharge other debts.
- Filing pro se is possible but Chapter 13 is genuinely hard to do without help. Ask legal aid or the local bankruptcy court about pro bono bankruptcy clinics. The Chapter 13 filing fee (~$313) can be paid in installments.

## Which should you choose?

- **If you were never properly served or have a genuine defense** → pursue the 60(B) motion *and* a stay, fast.
- **If you simply fell behind but have income to catch up** → Chapter 13 is the more reliable path.
- These aren't mutually exclusive — you can file the 60(B) motion now and keep bankruptcy as the backstop if the sale date approaches and the stay hasn't been granted. In Ohio you also retain rights up until the court **confirms** the sale, but don't count on post-sale remedies; act before the sale date.

## Do these things this week

1. Call the sheriff's office — get the sale date.
2. Call Legal Aid Society of Southwest Ohio and Save the Dream Ohio.
3. Call Wells Fargo's loss mitigation department — ask about a modification and whether they'll agree to postpone the sale (lenders sometimes do while reviewing a workout application).
4. Gather your papers: the note and mortgage, payment records, all court filings, and proof of how (or whether) you were served.

You have a real chance to keep the house, but only if you move in the next few days rather than weeks.

### Question

Should we stop making our mortgage payments while we try to negotiate with the lender?

### Model response

<div class="response">

**No — if you can afford to make payments, don't stop.** In your situation, stopping payments would hurt you in almost every direction. Here's why:

## Why continuing to pay (or save) helps you

**1. Every missed payment digs the hole deeper.** To keep the house, you'll eventually need to cure the arrears — whether through reinstatement, a loan modification, or a Chapter 13 plan. Every month you skip adds another payment, plus late fees, interest, and the lender's mounting legal costs, to the amount you must catch up.

**2. Your Chapter 13 option depends on it.** If bankruptcy becomes your backstop, a Chapter 13 plan requires you to (a) resume full regular mortgage payments *and* (b) pay off the arrears over 3–5 years. The smaller the arrearage and the more cash you have on hand, the more feasible and affordable your plan. Homeowners who kept paying — or kept saving — are the ones whose plans get confirmed.

**3. It strengthens your negotiating position.** A loan modification is essentially Wells Fargo deciding whether you're a good bet going forward. Demonstrated ability and willingness to pay is the single most persuasive fact in a loss mitigation review. The idea that lenders "only negotiate if you stop paying" doesn't apply to you — you're *already* in default with a judgment. There's nothing left to prove on that front.

**4. It helps your 60(B) motion.** A judge deciding whether to vacate a judgment and stay a sale is more sympathetic to homeowners who are visibly trying to pay their debt than to those who stopped entirely.

## One practical wrinkle: Wells Fargo may not accept your payments

Once a loan is accelerated and in foreclosure judgment, servicers often **refuse partial payments** — they may return your check or park it in a "suspense account" rather than apply it. They typically only accept either full reinstatement (all arrears + fees at once) or an agreed workout amount. So:

- **Try to pay.** Call the loss mitigation department and ask whether they'll accept ongoing monthly payments and what the current **reinstatement amount** is (get it in writing).
- **If they refuse the money, don't spend it.** Put every mortgage payment you would have made into a separate savings account, every month, without fail.

That saved money becomes your most powerful asset. It can fund:
- A lump-sum reinstatement if you get close to the payoff of arrears (possibly with Save the Dream Ohio covering part),
- The down payment on a modification or repayment plan,
- Your Chapter 13 plan payments and filing costs, or
- A bankruptcy attorney if it comes to that — which is money very well spent.

## What *not* to do

- **Don't stop paying to "get their attention."** You already have their attention — a judgment and a scheduled sale.
- **Don't let a verbal promise substitute for a signed agreement.** If Wells Fargo says they'll postpone the sale during modification review, get it in writing, and confirm with the sheriff's office that the sale was actually withdrawn. Sales have gone forward despite pending modification talks (this is called "dual tracking," and it's restricted under 12 C.F.R. § 1024.41 — another reason to document everything).
- **Don't drain this money on anything else**, including paying down credit cards. The house comes first; unsecured debts can be dealt with later (or discharged in bankruptcy).

**Bottom line:** Keep the money flowing toward the mortgage — into Wells Fargo's hands if they'll take it, into a dedicated savings account if they won't. Either way, document every payment attempt in writing. And keep pushing on legal aid and Save the Dream Ohio this week; the combination of saved funds plus state assistance is exactly how many Ohio families in your position have reinstated their loans and kept their homes.
