### Model response

<div class="response">

What you describe is likely a **Colorado Rule 120 proceeding**. The lender is asking the district court for an **Order Authorizing Sale** under the “power of sale” in the deed of trust. It is not an ordinary lawsuit, so the usual pleading generally is **not an Answer**.

### What to file

For **each motion, case number, and property**, file:

> **Response and Objection to Motion for Order Authorizing Sale Under C.R.C.P. 120 and Request for Hearing**

File it in the court shown on the motion, under the same case number. The response should:

1. Identify you and your ownership interest.
2. Admit or deny the important allegations.
3. State specific reasons the sale should not be authorized, such as:
   - Payments were made or the stated default/amount is incorrect.
   - The lender failed to credit payments or comply with required notices.
   - The moving party has not shown it is entitled to enforce the debt or deed of trust.
   - The deed of trust does not cover the property identified in the motion.
   - The second property was not pledged as security for this loan.
   - The legal description, borrower, loan, or property is incorrect.
   - You or your husband are protected by the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act.
4. Attach supporting documents, including deeds of trust, payment records, notices, correspondence, and relevant portions of closing documents.
5. Specifically request a hearing and ask the court to deny the motion.
6. Include a **certificate of service** showing that you sent a copy to the lender’s attorney.

A simple title and request could read:

> “Respondents object to the Motion for Order Authorizing Sale and request a hearing under C.R.C.P. 120. There is not a reasonable probability that a default justifying sale exists because: [list specific facts]. Respondents further dispute that the deed of trust secures the property described in the motion. Respondents request that the motion be denied.”

### Important deadline

The notice should give a response deadline, commonly tied to the scheduled hearing. If no timely response is filed, the court may authorize the sale without a contested hearing. Because July 29 and September 10 may already have passed, immediately:

- Check each case on the Colorado court docket or call the court clerk.
- Ask whether an order authorizing sale has already been entered.
- Contact the county public trustee to determine whether a sale is scheduled.
- File a separate response in each proceeding; do not assume one response covers both properties.

Each spouse should sign the response or file separately. A self-represented spouse generally cannot act as the other spouse’s lawyer.

### If an order has already been entered

A Rule 120 order generally is not handled through an ordinary appeal and does not finally determine all defenses. Depending on the circumstances, possible filings may include:

- A motion to set aside or vacate the order based on lack of notice or another procedural defect; and/or
- A separate civil complaint challenging the foreclosure, accompanied by a **motion for temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction under C.R.C.P. 65** before the sale occurs.

Those steps are substantially more complicated, and merely filing a lawsuit does **not** automatically stop a foreclosure sale.

Call the **Colorado Foreclosure Hotline at 1-877-601-HOPE (4673)** and **Colorado Legal Services** immediately. A HUD-approved housing counselor may also help with cure, modification, or other loss-mitigation options. Bring both motions, both deeds of trust, the promissory note, payment history, all default notices, and any scheduled sale notices.

### Question

The company is acting unfair and I think some of their employees might be doing things illegally. Would it be a good idea to add on a civil suit to show the lender is not very legitimate?

### Model response

<div class="response">

Usually, **no—not merely to show that the lender is “not legitimate.”** A Colorado C.R.C.P. 120 foreclosure proceeding has a narrow purpose: whether there is a reasonable probability of a default or other circumstance permitting sale. It generally is **not the place to add counterclaims for damages**, and filing a separate civil case does **not automatically stop the foreclosure**.

### When alleged misconduct belongs in the Rule 120 response

Include the conduct if you have specific facts showing that:

- Payments were misapplied or the default amount is false;
- Documents, signatures, assignments, or declarations used in the foreclosure were forged or materially inaccurate;
- The company seeking foreclosure is not entitled to enforce the note or deed of trust;
- The deed of trust does not cover one of the properties;
- Required notices were not sent; or
- A legally enforceable loss-mitigation restriction prevents the sale.

State facts rather than conclusions. For example:

> “The motion relies on a payment history stating that no payment was received after March 1. Respondents’ bank records show that the servicer accepted payments of $___ on April 3 and $___ on May 2. Those payments do not appear in the payment history attached to the motion.”

Or, if supported by evidence:

> “Respondents dispute the accuracy and authenticity of the assignment dated ____. The assignment identifies ____ as the signer, but [describe the specific discrepancy and supporting document]. Respondents request that the moving party produce competent evidence showing its entitlement to enforce the obligation.”

Avoid writing that employees “committed fraud,” “forged documents,” or committed crimes unless you can identify the particular document, statement, person, date, why it is false, and the evidence supporting that assertion.

### When a separate civil suit may be appropriate

A separate lawsuit may be warranted if you have a recognized legal claim, supporting evidence, and resulting injury—for example, certain violations involving servicing errors, false representations, debt collection, discrimination, or a breach of the loan documents. The proper defendants may differ from the lender; they could include the loan owner, servicer, trustee, or another company.

If a sale is imminent, stopping it ordinarily requires more than filing a complaint. You may need a **motion for temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction under C.R.C.P. 65**, supported by a verified complaint or affidavits. You generally must demonstrate matters such as a substantial likelihood of success, immediate irreparable injury, and why equitable relief is justified. The court may require a bond. Recording a notice of lis pendens or submitting complaints to regulators also does not automatically stop the sale.

Do **not** file a suit solely to delay the foreclosure or pressure the company. Unsupported allegations can be dismissed and can expose you to costs, attorney fees, or sanctions.

### A safer approach now

1. Put every concrete foreclosure-related fact in each Rule 120 objection.
2. Attach payment records, notices, the note, deeds of trust, assignments, and correspondence.
3. Ask for a hearing and require the moving party to establish the default and its right to enforce the deed of trust.
4. Preserve unrelated claims with language such as:

> “Respondents reserve any claims and defenses outside the limited scope of this Rule 120 proceeding. No such claim is waived by this response.”

5. Send a written **notice of error/request for information** to the mortgage servicer if there are servicing errors, using the special address identified on the mortgage statement for such requests. Keep proof of delivery.
6. Report documented misconduct, where appropriate, to the **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau**, **Colorado Attorney General**, or the relevant Colorado mortgage regulator—but do not assume a complaint will postpone the sale.
7. Immediately obtain the docket and sale date. If an order has already been entered or the sale is close, contact **Colorado Legal Services** and the **Colorado Foreclosure Hotline, 1-877-601-4673**, because emergency-injunction procedure is difficult and time-sensitive.

The strongest argument is not that the company is generally unfair; it is that a **specific, provable act means the claimed default, amount, lien, or right to sell this particular property is incorrect**.
